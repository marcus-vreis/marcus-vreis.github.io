Luggage theft in airport waiting areas is not an identity problem — it is an
**object custody** problem. The operational question is not *"who is this
person?"*, but *"is the person walking away with the bag the same one who set
it down?"*. That reframing eliminates facial recognition, biometric storage
and autonomous accusation: a pre-commit hook explicitly rejects `insightface`,
`deepface` and `dlib`. Because the base rate makes a binary alarm
mathematically useless (~1 real theft per 30,000 custody events), the system
reduces ~600,000 daily custody events to a ranked review queue of clips for a
human operator.

The architecture is split across a strict seam in the middle: everything left
of `GroundPlane` (`VideoSource → Detector → GroundPlane`) speaks **pixels**;
everything right of it (`BagRegistry → CustodyFSM → PartyManager → FlagEngine → AlertQueue`)
consumes only **metres and seconds** `(x, y, t)`. The logic half cannot tell
whether trajectories came from a real camera or a 12-line synthetic scenario —
which exposed five chained occlusion and ownership defects (where a 5% person
detection failure rate cost 40% of ownership links when ownership was decided
in a single frame).

The evaluation harness also refuses to manufacture numbers from mismatched
benchmarks: under the 25 s PETS2007 protocol, `evaluate.py` inspects CAVIAR's
XML annotations, finds that the longest annotated abandonment lasts **13.3 s**,
and exits 1 refusing to compute `P_miss` — because emitting nothing at 25 s is
the correct behaviour, and a miss rate computed there would measure clip
length rather than the system.
